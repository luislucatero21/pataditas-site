Baby moving less than usual? What to do now, and what happens at the check

By Luis Angel Lucatero Villanueva, founder of Kaelux, who makes Pataditas · Updated November 10, 2026 · Sources checked November 10, 2026

If your baby is moving less than usual, has stopped moving or the pattern has changed, contact your midwife, doctor or maternity unit now, day or night. Do not wait until tomorrow, and do not rely on an app, a kick count or a home heartbeat monitor to decide. If you cannot reach them, go to the nearest hospital. This page is general information, not medical advice.

This page is for the moment you notice a change. It brings together what the NHS and RCOG (UK), Count the Kicks (USA), the IMSS (Mexico) and Brazil’s Ministry of Health say to do, and what usually happens when you are checked.

Quick answer

  • Call now, not tomorrow. Every source on this page says a reduction or change in movements needs to be checked straight away.
  • Do not use a home doppler to reassure yourself: the NHS says hearing a heartbeat does not mean your baby is well.
  • After 28 weeks, the RCOG says you will usually be asked about the movements, have your baby’s heartbeat checked and monitored (usually for at least 20 minutes), and sometimes have an ultrasound scan.
  • If it happens again, call again, no matter how many times (RCOG).

What should I do if my baby is moving less than usual?

Contact your midwife, doctor or maternity unit straight away. The NHS says to call immediately if your baby is moving less than usual, you cannot feel your baby moving any more, or the usual pattern changes, and not to wait until the next day, even in the middle of the night.

Count the Kicks says to go to the nearest hospital if you cannot reach your provider. The RCOG says never to go to sleep ignoring a reduction or change in your baby’s movements. In Mexico, the IMSS says to go to hospital immediately, whatever the day or time. In Brazil, the Ministry of Health says to seek care immediately at your health unit, your reference maternity unit or an emergency service, or to call SAMU on 192.

Why do reduced movements matter?

Because a baby who is not well may move less. The NHS says less movement can be a sign of a problem, that the sooner this is found out the better, and that this could save your baby’s life.

Count the Kicks says research shows that a change in a baby’s movement in the third trimester is sometimes the earliest or only sign of distress. That is why every source on this page says to act on a change rather than wait to see whether it passes.

Can I check with a home doppler or an app first?

No. The NHS says not to use a home doppler (heartbeat listening kit) to check on your baby, because it is not reliable, and that even if you hear a heartbeat this does not mean your baby is well. The RCOG also says not to rely on home kits.

No app can check your baby either. Pataditas only records the taps you make; it cannot tell whether your baby is well, and a count in the app is never a reason to wait.

Do babies move less near the end of pregnancy?

No. Count the Kicks says babies do not kick less or run out of room at the end of pregnancy: the type of movement may change, with more jabs and fewer rolls, but your baby should keep the same pattern up to and during labour. The RCOG says the number of movements tends to increase until 32 weeks and then stay about the same.

So a reduction late in pregnancy is not something to put down to lack of space. Call your midwife, doctor or maternity unit.

Should I have a cold or sugary drink to get my baby moving first?

No. Count the Kicks says research has moved away from using sugary drinks, juice, sweets or ice water to get a baby to move, and that you should call your provider if your baby is not moving like normal.

What happens when you go in to be checked?

It depends on how many weeks pregnant you are and on your hospital. The RCOG (UK) says that after 28 weeks you will be asked about your baby’s movements and have a full antenatal check, including your baby’s heartbeat, and that your baby’s heart rate will usually be monitored for at least 20 minutes.

The RCOG says an ultrasound scan to check your baby’s growth and the fluid around them may be arranged if your womb measures smaller than expected, if your pregnancy has risk factors for stillbirth, or if the monitoring is normal but you still feel the movements are less than usual. Between 24 and 28 weeks it describes a heartbeat check and a full antenatal check; if you have not felt your baby move by 24 weeks, a heartbeat check and possibly a scan. Mexico’s IMSS guideline recommends an assessment that includes a non-stress test (monitoring your baby’s heart rate) and/or measuring the amniotic fluid.

What should I say when I call?

These are practical tips from us, not from the sources above: say how many weeks pregnant you are, when you last felt your baby move in their usual way, and what has changed (fewer movements, weaker movements, or none). Mention anything else you have noticed, such as bleeding, fluid leaking or pain.

Take your pregnancy notes or prenatal card if you go in. If you log movements in an app, you can mention it, but the staff will check your baby directly: an app record is not a test.

What if my baby moves less again after I have been checked?

Call again straight away. The RCOG says that if your baby has another episode of reduced movements you must contact your midwife or maternity unit again immediately, and never hesitate to do so, no matter how many times it happens.

Can Pataditas tell me if my baby is OK?

No. Pataditas is an iPhone and Apple Watch app that records the movements you feel, so you have a record of your own baby’s pattern to talk about with your midwife or doctor. It is not a medical device, does not diagnose anything and cannot tell whether your baby is well.

Its 10-movements-in-2-hours grouping is the app’s own feature, not a safety threshold. If you notice a change, call, whatever the app shows. Read how counting advice differs between countries.
