Pataditas / Timing contractions

How to time contractions, and when to call your midwife or go to hospital

By Luis Angel Lucatero Villanueva, founder of Kaelux, who makes Pataditas · Updated November 10, 2026 · Sources checked November 10, 2026

Call your midwife, doctor or maternity unit now, day or night, if your waters break, you are bleeding, your baby is moving less than usual, or you are less than 37 weeks pregnant and think labour has started. Do not wait for your contractions to reach a pattern or for an app. This page is general information, not medical advice.

Timing contractions helps you tell your midwife, doctor or maternity unit what is happening. This page explains how to time them and what the NHS (UK), US government health sources and health bodies in Brazil and Mexico say about when to get in touch. Your own care team’s instructions come first.

Quick answer

  • Time from the start of one contraction to the start of the next (how often), and how long each one lasts (March of Dimes).
  • The NHS says to call when you have regular contractions every 5 minutes or more often, and to call urgently if one lasts longer than 2 minutes or you have 6 or more in 10 minutes.
  • MedlinePlus (USA) says to contact your provider for regular, painful contractions every 5 to 10 minutes for 60 minutes.
  • The rules differ between countries and hospitals, so ask your care team before labour which rule they want you to follow, and call sooner if you are unsure or worried.

How do you time contractions?

Note when each contraction starts and when it ends. How long it lasts is the duration; the time from the start of one contraction to the start of the next is how often they are coming, which is how March of Dimes says to measure it. Also note how strong each one feels and whether they continue when you rest, drink water or move around.

Keep timing for a while rather than judging from two or three contractions: the NHS and MedlinePlus describe labour contractions as coming regularly and getting longer, stronger and closer together over time. In Pataditas you start the timer when a contraction begins and stop it when it ends; the app shows the last duration, the time since the previous contraction began, and how many contractions you logged in the last hour.

How far apart should contractions be before you call or go to hospital?

It depends on where you live and on what your care team told you. The NHS (UK) says to call your midwife or maternity unit for guidance if you think you are in labour or have regular contractions every 5 minutes or more often. MedlinePlus (USA) says to contact your provider for regular, painful contractions every 5 to 10 minutes for 60 minutes, or contractions lasting 60 to 90 seconds.

Brazil’s Ministry of Health says the time to go to the maternity unit is the active phase, when contractions come every 2 to 3 minutes and last more than 30 seconds. Ask your midwife or doctor before labour starts which pattern should make you call. The NHS adds that you can call your midwife or maternity unit any time you are unsure or worried.

Which signs mean call now, whatever your contractions are doing?

The NHS says to call your midwife or maternity unit urgently if your waters break, you have vaginal bleeding, your baby is moving less than usual, you are less than 37 weeks pregnant and think you might be in labour, any contraction lasts longer than 2 minutes, or you are having 6 or more contractions every 10 minutes. It says not to wait until the next day, even in the middle of the night.

If you think your baby is coming now and you have a strong urge to push, the NHS says to call an ambulance (999 in the UK; use your local emergency number). If your waters break, the NHS says to tell your midwife immediately if the fluid smells or is coloured, or if you are losing blood.

How can I tell Braxton Hicks from labour contractions?

Braxton Hicks, or practice, contractions are usually short, irregular and painless or only uncomfortable, and they do not build up, according to the NHS and MedlinePlus. Labour contractions come regularly, get closer together, become longer and stronger over time, and do not stop whatever you do.

March of Dimes notes that false labour contractions can still be painful, and that sometimes the only way to tell the difference is for your provider to check your cervix. If you are not sure, call.

What is the 5-1-1 rule, and how does Pataditas use it?

5-1-1 describes contractions about 5 minutes apart, each lasting about 1 minute, for about 1 hour. Pataditas can alert you when your timings match that pattern. It is the app’s own setting, not your hospital’s rule: the NHS, MedlinePlus and Brazil’s Ministry of Health each use different thresholds, listed above.

The app does not know how many weeks pregnant you are, your history or what your care team told you. The app itself says you do not need to wait for the 5-1-1 pattern to contact your care team. Use the alert as a prompt to check your own instructions, never as a reason to wait.

What happens if I go in too early?

You may be checked and then advised to go home for a while. The NHS says this can happen if your labour is not yet established, and that the early (latent) phase can take hours or sometimes days. Mexico’s IMSS guideline likewise admits women to the labour ward from the active phase.

Being checked when you are unsure is never wasted. Brazil’s Ministry of Health puts it simply: you do not need to be certain, because being assessed is part of safe care.

Kick counter or contraction timer: which do I need?

They answer different questions. A kick counter records the baby movements you feel during the second half of pregnancy; a contraction timer records when your womb tightens and relaxes, usually near the end. Pataditas includes both, free, on iPhone.

Neither replaces a check. If your baby moves less than usual, call straight away, whether or not you are having contractions: MedlinePlus lists decreased fetal movement among the reasons to contact your provider right away.
